## 1. Scope, purpose, relation to other standards

**Purpose.** Methods for analysing life data (time to failure, cycles, mechanical stress, etc.) drawn from a
Weibull distribution, for a random sample operating under test or in-service conditions, in order to estimate
reliability performance of the population. Applies to strength/life-type data that are **independently,
identically distributed** (to be tested or assumed; cf. IEC 60300-3-5).

**Provides:** numerical and graphical methods to (a) plot the data, (b) perform a goodness-of-fit test,
(c) estimate parameters of the **two- or three-parameter** Weibull distribution, (d) plot confidence limits;
guidance on interpreting the plot in terms of risk vs. time, failure modes, weak/mixed populations, time to
first failure / minimum endurance.

**History.** 2nd edition (2008) cancels and replaces 1st edition (1997). Main changes: title shortened to
"Weibull analysis"; addition of methods for both **analytical and graphical** solutions.

**Normative references.**
- IEC 60050-191:1990 — IEV Part 191: Dependability and quality of service (terms/definitions/symbols)
- IEC 60300-3-5:2001 — Dependability management 3-5: Reliability test conditions and statistical test principles
- IEC 61810-2 — Electromechanical elementary relays — Part 2: Reliability (contains hazard-plotting examples)
- ISO 2854:1976 — Statistical interpretation of data (χ² and Student-t fractile tables, Tables IIa/III/IV)
- ISO 3534-1:2006 — Statistics — Vocabulary and symbols

**Definitions (3.1).** censoring (terminating a test after a given duration or a given number of failures);
suspended item (test curtailed without relevant failure; early/late suspension); life test; non-repairable /
repairable item; operating time; relevant failure; reliability test; time to failure; time between failures;
**B life / L percentile** = age at which a given percentage of items have failed (e.g. B10 = 10 % failed;
B50 = median time to failure).

**Abbreviations/symbols (3.2–3.3).** CDF, PDF, MLE (maximum likelihood estimation), MRR (median rank
regression), MTTF, ASIC, BGA.
- `t` time (generic: time, cycles, distance, stress applications)
- `η` characteristic life (scale parameter); `β` shape parameter; `t0` starting point / failure-free time
- `r²` coefficient of determination; `f(t)` pdf; `F(t)` cdf; `h(t)` hazard function; `λ(t)` instantaneous
  failure rate; `H(t)` cumulative hazard; `F1, F2, F3` number of failures with failure mode 1/2/3.

## 2. The Weibull distribution (Clause 5)

### 2.1 Two-parameter Weibull

PDF (Eq. 1):
$$f(t) = \frac{\beta}{\eta}\left(\frac{t}{\eta}\right)^{\beta-1} \exp\!\left[-\left(\frac{t}{\eta}\right)^{\beta}\right], \qquad t \ge 0$$

CDF (Eq. 2):
$$F(t) = 1 - \exp\left[-(t/\eta)^{\beta}\right]$$

Instantaneous failure rate / hazard (Eq. 3):
$$\lambda(t) = h(t) = \frac{f(t)}{1-F(t)} = \frac{\beta}{\eta}\left(\frac{t}{\eta}\right)^{\beta-1}$$

Survival (reliability):
$$R(t) = 1 - F(t) = \exp\left[-(t/\eta)^{\beta}\right]$$

**Shape parameter β** = rate of change of instantaneous failure rate with time (infant mortality, random,
wear-out); selects the member of the Weibull family (Figure 1 shows PDF shapes for η = 1: β = 0.5, 1, 3.44;
β = 3.44 looks approximately normal except for the tails).
- β = 1.0 → identical to the **exponential** distribution; λ(t) constant = 1/η.
- β > 1.0 → increasing instantaneous failure rate (wear-out).
- β < 1.0 → decreasing instantaneous failure rate (early failures / infant mortality).

**Scale parameter η (characteristic life):** time at which 63.2 % of items are expected to fail, for *every*
β, because with t = η (Eq. 4):
$$F(\eta) = 1 - e^{-1} = 1 - 1/e = 0.632$$

Derived quantities (used throughout the standard):
- B_p life (B life, L percentile): age at which p × 100 % of items have failed,
  $$B_p = \eta\left[-\ln(1-p)\right]^{1/\beta}$$
- Mean time to failure (Eq. 19): `m̂ = η̂ · Γ(1 + 1/β̂)`.
- Cumulative hazard (Eq. 12): `H(t) = (t/η)^β`.

### 2.2 Three-parameter Weibull

CDF (Eq. 5, and H.1):
$$F(t) = 1 - \exp\left[-\left(\frac{t - t_0}{\eta}\right)^{\beta}\right], \qquad t \ge t_0$$

`t0` = **failure-free time, location parameter, minimum life**, origin of the distribution (always less than the
first failure time). Effects: shelf-life before first failure; minimum endurance (also as stress/strain scale in
material data: fracture, creep). Indicator of a needed location parameter: **convex/concave curvature** of the
2-parameter Weibull plot (see §7). The three criteria before using a 3-parameter Weibull (8.5):
(a) the Weibull plot shall show concave curvature; (b) a physical explanation of why failures cannot occur
before t0 shall exist; (c) larger sample, at least **21 failures** (8–10 acceptable if prior knowledge supports
t0). Concave downward plots (most common) → subtract t0 (shift origin right); concave upward → negative
t0 (add t0; e.g. items aged/wear-out stressed before test, shelf-aged spares). Estimating t0: fit a curve
through the data and extrapolate down to the time axis (intersection ≈ t0); or optimize by re-plotting until
linear (Figures 7→8 example: β 1.870/η 57.656 (2-par) becomes β 0.873, η 297.337, threshold 2.9997 months).
After shifting, the ordinate scale and η live in the *t0 domain*; add t0 back for real time. A failure-free
period must not be assumed without technical justification; the 3-parameter fit is always better than 2-
parameter simply because it is a more complex model.

## 3. Data considerations (Clause 6)

- **Data types:** "age" may be operating time, starts/stops, landings, low-cycle fatigue cycles, mileage, shelf
  time, cycles/time at high stress or temperature. All called "time" in the standard.
- **Time to first failure:** interpretations — time to first failure of a repairable item; time to failure of a
  non-repairable item; time from new to each failure of a repairable system (with "bad-as-old" renewal
  assumption); time to first failure after scheduled maintenance.
- **Material characteristics:** creep, stress rupture, breakage, fatigue often plotted on Weibull paper with
  stress/cycles/load/temperature as abscissa.
- **Sample size:** estimation possible from as few as **2 failures**, but the uncertainty is excessive and
  cannot confirm applicability of the Weibull model; confidence limits must always be computed/plotted
  (§11.3 for small samples).
- **Censoring classes (6.5):** complete data (all times observed); time-censored (test stopped at specified
  time T before all fail); failure-censored (stop after a specified number of failures); items failing in a
  different failure mode count as suspensions/censored items for the mode of interest. Early suspension =
  suspended before first failure; late suspension = after last failure; between = random/progressive. Further
  censoring discussion in IEC 60300-3-5.

## 4. Probability plotting (Clause 7; Annexes E, F, I)

### 4.1 Ranking and plotting positions (7.2.1)

1. Rank failure times from lowest to highest: X-coordinate = ranked time `t`; Y-coordinate = **median rank**
   `F(t)` in %.
2. Median ranks (Annex C, tables of 5 %, 50 %, 95 % ranks for N ≤ 30) are the quantiles of the Beta
   distribution; generate with the spreadsheet function:
   `BETAINV(C, J, N - J + 1)` — C = confidence level (0.5 for median), J = rank order, N = sample size.
   (Exact median rank = median of Beta(i, N−i+1); i.e. `BETAINV(0.5, i, N−i+1)`.)
3. **Benard's approximation** (Eq. 6) when tables unavailable:
   $$F_i = \frac{i - 0.3}{N + 0.4}\times 100\ \%$$
   (NOTE: mostly used for N ≤ 30; for N > 30 neglect correction and use `F_i = (i/N)×100 %`.)
   Example (Table 2, rivet failures, N = 5): ranks 12.94, 31.38, 50.00, 68.62, 87.06 for times 30, 49, 82, 90, 96.
4. Median estimate preferred over mean for skewed (life) distributions. Median-rank tables also serve
   log-normal, normal, extreme-value papers.
5. NOTE: if two data points have the same (tied) time, plot them at *different* median-rank values
   (sequential order numbers).

### 4.2 Suspended/censored data — adjusted ranks (7.2.3)

Cannot be ignored. Procedure:
a) rank **all** times (failures and suspensions) earliest→latest with reverse rank = N+1−order;
b) compute **adjusted rank** for each *failure* (Eq. 7, no separate rank increments needed):
   $$\text{Adj}_i = \frac{(\text{Reverse rank})\times(\text{Previous adjusted rank}) + (N+1)}{(\text{Reverse rank}) + 1}$$
   (first failure's "previous adjusted rank" = 0; suspensions are not plotted);
c) median rank from the adjusted rank `i'` via Benard: `F_i = (i' − 0.3)/(N + 0.4) × 100 %` (Eq. 8 example:
   adjusted rank 1.125 with N = 8 → (1.125−0.3)/(8+0.4)·100 = **9.82 %**);
d) plot failure times (x) vs. median ranks (y) on Weibull paper; e) read η at B63.2; f) estimate β by slope or
   the printed β scales on the paper; g) interpret.

Table 3 worked example (N = 8: suspensions at 10, 45, 100; failures 30, 49, 82, 90, 96):
Adj(30) = [7×0 + (8+1)]/(7+1) = 1.125 → 9.8 %; Adj(49) = [5×1.125 + 9]/(5+1) = 2.438 → 25.5 %;
Adj(82) = 3.750 → 41.1 %; Adj(90) = 5.063 → 56.7 %; Adj(96) = 6.375 → 72.3 %.
Ties: sequential rank numbers; repeat the procedure for the rest of the failures after a later suspension.

**Equivalent recursion (Annex E.2):** with adjusted failure number `i` updated at each failure event `j`:
$$i_{(j)} = i_{(j-1)} + \frac{(N+1) - i_{(j-1)}}{1 + (N - j)} \qquad\text{(suspension events are skipped in the update chain)}$$
then `F_i(t) = (i − 0.3)/(n + 0.4)`, `x = ln(t_i)`, `y = ln{ln[1/(1−F_i(t))]}`.

Benard's approximation is sufficiently accurate even with suspensions, for plotting and for estimating
parameters.

### 4.3 Weibull probability plot construction (7.2.2, 7.2.4; Annexes E, F, I)

Built by the linearized form (double-log transform, Eq. I.2/I.3):
$$\ln\left[-\ln(1-F(t))\right] = \beta \ln t - \beta \ln \eta$$
i.e. straight line `y = m x + c` with `y = ln ln [1/(1−F(t))]`, `x = ln t`, slope `m = β` (Eq. I.4), intercept
`c = −β ln η`, hence (Eq. I.5) `η = e^{−intercept/β}`. Weibull paper = log-log-reciprocal ordinate against log
abscissa (Annex F shows example paper; Annex I gives construction tables: ordinate `F(t)` 0.001→lnln⁻¹-values
−6.91…+6.91; abscissa ln t).

Three construction routes: Weibull probability paper; spreadsheet (Annex E); commercial software.

**Spreadsheet regression setup (Annex E.1, Table E.1).** Columns: i, t_i, F_i(t) = (i−0.3)/(n+0.4),
X = ln(t_i), Y = ln(1/ln(1−F_i(t))). Two regression variants:
- **X on Y (standard; commercial software default)** — regress the variable with more uncertainty (t) against
  the one with less: `(1/β)·ln[−ln(1−F)] + ln η = ln t`. Example: `y = 1.1115x + 5.1265`, R² = 0.9824 →
  β = 1/1.1115 = 0.8997, η = exp(5.1265) = 168.42 h.
- **Y on X (special)** — `ln[−ln(1−F)] = β ln t − β ln η`. Example: `y = 0.8839x − 4.5403` → β = 0.8839,
  η = exp(4.5403/0.8839) = 170.15 h.
(`LINEST` or the plot's displayed trendline + R² may be used. Results of the two variants are similar.)

**MRR definition:** median rank regression = median-rank plotting positions + least-squares regression on the
Weibull paper as fitting criterion.

### 4.4 Checking the fit (7.2.5)

If data cluster around a straight line → evidence for the distribution. Subjective straight-line-by-eye gives an
indication. Name-checked statistical tests: Chi-squared, Kolmogorov–Smirnov, Nancy Mann's tests — but the
**standard's own measure is the coefficient of determination r²** (square of the correlation coefficient, Eq. 9):

$$r^2 = \frac{\left(N\sum x_i y_i - \sum x_i \sum y_i\right)^2}{\left[N\sum x_i^2 - \left(\sum x_i\right)^2\right]\left[N\sum y_i^2 - \left(\sum y_i\right)^2\right]}$$

where x_i, y_i = median rank and failure time pairs (per Annex B usage: between ln t and lnln coordinates),
N = sample size. Interpretation: r² = proportion of variation explained by the Weibull hypothesis; →1 good,
→0 poor; use visual inspection as the primary check (Annex B cites a ~90 % critical value of **90.3 %** at the
10 % level for its n/r; commercial software/r² tables give critical values).

### 4.5 Hazard plotting (7.3)

Alternative graphical estimator using the cumulative hazard. Relations (Eqs. 10–14):
$$h(t) = \frac{f(t)}{1-F(t)}, \qquad H(t) = \int_0^t h(t')\,dt' = -\ln(1-F(t)), \qquad F(t) = 1 - e^{-H(t)}$$
For the Weibull: `H(t) = (t/η)^β`, so
$$\ln H(t) = \beta \ln t - \beta \ln \eta$$
— a straight line on ln–ln ("hazard") paper; nominal slope = β; **t = η when H(t) = 1**. (Natural or base-10
logs both work.)

Procedure: a) sort failures and suspensions together earliest→latest; b) for each failure, instantaneous
failure-rate estimate = `1/(number of items remaining after the previous failure or censoring)`; c) cumulative
sum of these = estimate of H(t); d) plot ln H (or H) against ln t (log-log paper); e) fit line; f) read β =
slope, η = t at H = 1. (Equivalent on ordinary Weibull paper via F(t) = 1−e^{−H(t)}; very easy in a
spreadsheet — Annex E.3/E.4; IEC 61810-2 also contains hazard-plotting examples.) Advantage over adjusted-
rank plotting: much simpler, comparable results; handles multiple failure modes by treating other modes'
failures as censorings.

## 5. Computational methods and goodness-of-fit (Clause 9; source basis Annex J)

### 5.1 Assumptions and limitations (9.2, 9.3)

Sample of `n` non-repairable items from the same population on test from t = 0; identical environment; failed
items not replaced; test stopped at time `T` with `r` failures; failure times `t1 < … < tr ≤ T` known exactly
(no interval data). NOTE: test time per item from time zero.
Limitations: procedures valid only for **≥ 10 relevant failures**; confidence intervals are *approximate*;
only **singly censored** data (not multiply censored). MLE is valid/approximately valid for large samples and
tolerates varied censoring mechanisms in principle, but this clause treats single censoring only.
Inputs: n; ordered t_i; significance level γ or confidence (1−γ).
Outputs: GoF accept/reject; point estimates + CIs of β, η; point estimate of MTTF; lower confidence limit
for B10; lower confidence limit for R(t).

### 5.2 Goodness-of-fit test (9.5; Mann–Scheuer–Fertig in Lawless' presentation, Blom approximations)

*The printed equations (15)–(16) are heavily glyph-corrupted in this text source; the reconstruction below
gives the exact structure; verify sub/superscripts against the published standard before critical use.*

- **Step 1:** sort r failure times; `x_i = ln(t_i)`, i = 1…r.
- **Step 2:** for i = 1…(r−1) compute the spacings `ℓ_i` (Eq. 15). These are normalized log-spacings
  `ℓ_i = (x_{i+1} − x_i)/(a_{i+1} − a_i)`, where `a_i` are the expected values of the standard extreme value
  (Gumbel) order statistics, approximated by Blom's (1958) method; in the printed formula the only inputs are
  n, i and the observed ln t's (no tables needed).
- **Step 3:** compute (Eq. 16), with ⌊·⌋ = largest integer ≤ argument and `l^2` = squared quantities:
  $$H = \frac{\sum_{i=\lfloor r/2\rfloor+1}^{r-1} \ell_i^{\,2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor r/2\rfloor} \ell_i^{\,2}}$$
- **Step 4:** reject the Weibull hypothesis at significance level γ·100 % if
  $$H \ge F_{\gamma}\big((2\lfloor r/2\rfloor - 2);\; 2\lfloor r/2\rfloor\big)$$
  (F-distribution fractiles, e.g. Table IV of ISO 2854); otherwise no evidence against Weibull → proceed.
  NOTE: on rejection, examine the plot for mixtures of populations, anomalous failure times, etc. (mostly
  outside the standard's scope).
- **Worked anchor (Annex B, n = 40, r = 20):** H = 0.36 vs F₀.₁(18; 20) = 1.81 → cannot reject at 10 %.

The test has power comparable to Shapiro–Brain (1987) W-test and Tiku's test, and can handle censored
samples (J.1).

### 5.3 MLE point estimates of β and η (9.6; Mann–Schafer–Singpurwalla form)

Solve numerically (convergence is fast; any root-finder works):

**Step 1 — β̂ solves (Eq. 17):**
$$\sum_{i=1}^{r} t_i^{\beta}\ln t_i + (n - r)\,T^{\beta}\ln T
\;=\;
\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r} t_i^{\beta} + (n-r)\,T^{\beta}\right)
\left[\frac{1}{\beta} + \frac{1}{r}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\ln t_i\right]$$

**Step 2 — η̂ from (Eq. 18):**
$$\hat{\eta} = \left[\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{r} t_i^{\hat\beta} + (n-r)\,T^{\hat\beta}}{r}\right]^{1/\hat\beta}$$

Validation: applying these to Annex B's data (n = 40, r = 20, T = t₂₀ = 68, failures 5, 10, 17, 32, 32, 33, 34,
36, 54, 55, 55, 58, 58, 61, 64, 65, 65, 66, 67, 68) yields β̂ = 2.0906, η̂ = 83.80 — the standard publishes
2.091 / 84. ✔ (Accompanying statistic L̂_i of Eq. 17 in the raw text contains the same variables:
(n−r), T^β, ln T, t_i^β, Σ ln t_i, r, β, and zeroes.)

### 5.4 Derived point estimates (9.7–9.9)

- **MTTF (Eq. 19):** `m̂ = η̂ · Γ(1 + 1/β̂)`; Γ from Table D.1 (linear interpolation acceptable). NOTE: if the
  lower CI limit of β ≥ 1 (wear-out), the η confidence interval is a rough surrogate for the MTTF interval
  since Γ(1+1/β) ∈ [0.88, 1] then; exponential-degradation tools only apply when β = 1; distribution is
  skewed, not symmetric.
- **B10 (Eq. 20):**
  $$\hat{B}_{10} = \hat{\eta}\left[\ln(1/0.9)\right]^{1/\hat\beta} = \hat{\eta}\left[-\ln 0.9\right]^{1/\hat\beta}$$
  (Annex B anchor: 28.63 MLE / 28.56 MRR ✔.)
- **Reliability for t ≤ T (Eq. 21):** `R̂(t) = exp[−(t/η̂)^{β̂}]`.

### 5.5 Software (9.10)

Commercial statistical/reliability packages give graphical and MLE estimates for single and multiple censored
data, suspensions and more general censoring.

## 6. Confidence intervals (Clause 10)

### 6.1 Interval estimation of β (10.1) — MLE, approximate

Let `q = r/n`, `ν = ⌊(r−1)C⌋` (⌊·⌋ = largest integer ≤ x; since (r−1)C is non-integer, χ² fractiles must be
computed or interpolated — ISO 2854 Table III or IEC 60605-4:2001 Table D.1).
- Eq. (22): `C = 2.14628 − 1.361119·q`
- Eq. (23): `β₁ = χ²_{1−γ/2}(ν)`
- Eq. (24): `β₂ = χ²_{γ/2}(ν)`
  (χ²_p(ν) = p-fractile of the χ² distribution with ν dof.)
- Eq. (25): `w₁ = β₁ / [((r−1)C)]^{1/β̂}` … Eq. (26): `w₂ = β₂ / [((r−1)C)]^{1/β̂}`
  *(the exponent placement and the “+q” token in the printed text are corrupted; per the Bain–Engelhardt
  pivot the interval is of the form β̂·w.)*
- Eq. (27): `(1−γ)·100 %` CI for β **= (β̂·w₁, β̂·w₂)** ordering so that the lower < β̂ < upper.

NOTE (10.1): the β interval can be used for **comparisons**: β > 1 = evidence of wear; β < 1 = infant
mortality; interval containing β = 1 → possible constant-failure-rate population (a formal constant-rate test
is in IEC 60605-6).

Annex B anchor (q = 0.5, γ = 0.10): MLE 90 % CI for β = [1.34, 2.742] (vs Bain&Engelhardt 1.34/2.73 ✔).

$$A_5 = 0.2445\,(1.78 - q)(2.25 + q)$$

### 6.3 MRR Beta-binomial bounds (10.3)

Derived from the median ranks via the Beta-binomial distribution (Johnson [14]); suspensions require
interpolation on the 5 % and 95 % rank tables (Annex C). Slightly **conservative** (interval too wide) compared
with Fisher-matrix and likelihood-ratio bounds. The 5 %/95 % ranks F_{i,(0.95)} and F_{i,(0.05)} are mapped to
time points on the Weibull line (Eqs. 39–40):
$$t_{i,(0.95)} = \hat\eta\left[\ln\frac{1}{1-F_{i,(0.95)}}\right]^{1/\beta} \qquad
t_{i,(0.05)} = \hat\eta\left[\ln\frac{1}{1-F_{i,(0.05)}}\right]^{1/\beta}$$

### 6.4 Fisher's matrix bounds (10.4)

Advantages over Beta-binomial; for moderate samples the apparent confidence is closer to the requested level
(though slightly optimistic). For **≤ 10 failures these bounds are too optimistic** (see ref. [20] Nelson).

## 7. MRR vs. MLE (Clause 11)

- **Graphical display:** MRR provides one (identifies bad fits, other distributions, multiple failure modes,
  mixtures, batches, outliers); MLE does not.
- **B-lives:** MRR more accurate for *low* percentiles (B-type) from small samples — important for safety,
  warranties, guarantees, contract obligations. MLE B-lives **optimistically biased** for small numbers of
  failures.
- **Small samples:** MRR failure forecasts usually more accurate (depends on shape/age distribution of
  suspensions). Advice: ≤ 20 data points (with/without censoring) → **X-on-Y MRR preferred**; < 10 points with
  prior β knowledge → **WeiBayes preferred**; otherwise **compute both MRR and MLE and compare** — close
  agreement + good r²/likelihood = assurance of correct model; large discrepancy → data not Weibull-modelled,
  investigate multiple populations.
- **Shape parameter:** MLE tends to **overestimate β** (slope too steep) in small samples; log-normal/normal
  MLE standard deviations similarly underestimated.
- **Confidence intervals:** likelihood-ratio intervals for MLE are rigorous (small-sample adjusted); use
  pivotal intervals for MRR.
- **Single failure:** MLE can give a solution with 1 failure + right/late suspensions (only solution when β
  unknown; large uncertainty); WeiBayes preferred if β known.
- **Mathematical rigour:** objection to least squares on rank regression: residual scatter is not uniform —
  lower end of line relatively over-weighted (acceptable to engineers since interest is in the lower tail,
  unacceptable to statisticians). MLE has attractive mathematical qualities.
- **Presentation:** keep it simple — MRR plots preferred; MLE plots with median-rank points are not
  recommended (invite comments about poor fit).

## 8. WeiBayes (Clause 12)

**Definition:** Weibull analysis with a **given β** (from historical failure data, prior experience, physics of
failure) → a single-parameter (η) Weibull. Works with and without failures; suspensions allowed.

**Method (Eq. 48):** with β assumed, the MLE of η is
$$\hat{\eta} = \left[\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} t_i^{\beta}}{r}\right]^{1/\beta}$$
where r = number of failed items, N = failures + suspensions, t = time/cycles. With β given and η computed, the
WeiBayes line is plotted on Weibull paper and read like any other (B lives, forecasts, reliability).

**Zero-failure WeiBayes (12.3):** assume first failure is imminent → set r = 1 (conservative; a lower one-sided
confidence estimate: with 63.2 % confidence the true Weibull lies to the right of the line, if β was correct).
Other confidence levels by fictitious denominators:

| Confidence | 50 % | 63.2 % | 90 % | 95 % | 99 % |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Denominator | 0.693 | 1.0 | 2.3 | 3.0 | 4.6 |

**With failures (12.4):** denominator = actual failures → η is an MLE estimate (invariant under
transformation → line, B-lives and reliability all MLE values; a "nominal" Weibull). Distinction: 5 units
tested 0-fail vs 4 suspensions + 1 failure give identical lines but different interpretations (one-sided
confidence bound vs MLE estimate). Lower C % bound of the MLE WeiBayes line via χ² (Eq. 49), r ≥ 1:
$$\eta_{C} = \hat{\eta}_{MLE}\left[\frac{\chi^{2}_{(2r+2),\,c}}{2r}\right]^{1/\beta}\quad\text{(dof reading corrupted at one level; see published standard)}$$

**Case study (12.5):** 15 fleet compressor failures gave β ≈ 5.0; 3 redesigned units tested 1600 h, 2900 h,
3100 h without failure (Eq. 50 gives the WeiBayes η; Figure 10: new design WeiBayes η ≈ 3486 h vs old design
η = 2132 h — the running text prints "4683", an apparent transposition of 3486). With 63 % confidence the
redesigned distribution lies to the right of the line → significantly better; not proof the failure mode is
eliminated. More zero-failure test time → line moves further right. A **Weibull slope library/data bank** is
strongly recommended.

Rationale (12.5 end): highly reliable items yield 0–1 failures, which cannot estimate a 2-/3-parameter Weibull;
with the failure-mode β known from previous tests, only η needs estimating — reduced testing cost without
loss of accuracy.

## 9. Sudden death testing (Clause 13)

Small subgroups (3–8, typically 4–10 per subgroup; **4 is the bearing-industry standard** giving L₁₆) run
simultaneously until **first failure in the subgroup**, then that subgroup stops (other items effectively
suspended at that same time). Ten sets of 4 → 10 failures + 30 suspensions. Trade-off: ↑ uncertainty, ↓↓ test
time.

**Procedure to estimate L_X (time to X % failed):** a) divide samples randomly into A subgroups of B
components per Table 4; b) test all subgroups; c) record first-failure time per subgroup; d) stop subgroup at
first failure; e) plot first-failure times on a Weibull diagram with the rest of each subgroup suspended at the
subgroup stop time; f) read L_X from the fitted line. The L_X point is *precise*; the rest of the curve and the
slope are less precise (many suspensions). Subgroups of 4 chosen also to save rigs.

Table 4 — precise median rank of the 1st failure in a subgroup of size B (→ L_X):

| B | rank | L_X | | B | rank | L_X |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 0.2929 | L30 | | 7 | 0.0943 | L9 |
| 3 | 0.2063 | L20 | | 8 | 0.0830 | L8 |
| 4 | 0.1591 | L16 | | 9 | 0.0741 | L7 |
| 5 | 0.1294 | L13 | | 10 | 0.0670 | L6 |
| 6 | 0.1091 | L10 | | 50 / 70 | 0.0138 / 0.00994 | L1 |

Confidence interval ≈ **50 % wider** than testing all to failure; L_X itself almost as reliable. Test-time
ratios: β = 1, subgroups sequential → 25 % of all-to-failure time; all subgroups simultaneous → ≈ 7 %.
Example (Table 5 chain data): 4 subgroups of 3 → first-of-3 mean rank = 1/(N+1) = 1/4 → B25; for β = 2.13,
B25 = 0.557·η and MTTF/η = 0.8858 ⇒ sudden-death test-time ratio ≈ (0.557×4)/(0.8858×12) ≈ 0.2 (80 % time
saving) with ≈ 27 % increase in uncertainty of B1 life (Fisher-matrix comparison, 10 sets of 3 vs all 12 to
failure). NOTE: data can also be analysed treating subgroups as single samples.

## 10. Interpretation of the plot (Clause 8)

### 10.1 The bathtub curve (8.1)
β vs. hazard over life: β < 1 (infant mortality/early life) → inadequate process control, inadequate burn-in/
RSS, production/mis-assembly/QC problems, overhaul problems, mixture of populations, run-in/wear-in; many
electronic components have β < 1 throughout useful life — **preventive maintenance inappropriate (old better
than new)**. β = 1 (random/useful-life period) → independent-of-time failure modes: random maintenance/human
errors, random overload, nature/FOD/lightning, mixtures of ≥ 3 modes with different β where none dominates;
PM inappropriate; β = 1 ≡ exponential (constant λ). β > 1 (wear-out) → wear, corrosion, crack propagation,
fatigue, moisture absorption, diffusion, evaporation, damage accumulation; design must keep these
insignificant over operational life; **3-parameter Weibull gives minimum time to first failure** — highly
advantageous when β > 1.

### 10.2 Masked modes (8.2)
Two competing modes with high β and very different η: small samples fail mostly in the lower-η mode; other
mode invisible until the first mode is eliminated (Figure 4, modes A/B).

### 10.3 Small samples (8.3)
Confidence limits widen strongly with small n (Figures 5/6: n = 10 vs n = 100 "90 % B-life bounds"); largest
uncertainty in the lower tail (small F(t)). **> 20 failures + suspensions required to differentiate Weibull
from other distributions.** Decisions from small samples should consider uncertainty; refine with more data
if possible.

### 10.4 Outliers (8.4)
Wild first/last points; investigate engineering causes (recording, test records, calibration); may indicate a
**weak population or process flaws** — often highly significant for reliability assurance.

### 10.5 Non-linear plots (8.5)
Curvature → possible t0 ≠ 0 (check for > 1 failure mode first; then Annex G mixture analysis). Concave down →
shift origin right (subtract t0); concave up → negative t0 (add). t0 < first failure time always.
**Other distributions:** log-normal data on Weibull paper are concave down (but straight on log-normal
paper); both 3-parameter Weibull and log-normal can model time-to-first-failure; use the best-describing
distribution. **Data inconsistencies/multimode clues (8.5.2):** mostly low-time failures with high-time
survivors → batch problem; batches of close serial numbers → batch problem; "dogleg" bend or cusps → mixture
of failure modes; suspect first/last point → data problems or another failure mode.

## 11. Mixed populations / multimodal (Clause 8, Annex G)

A dogleg Weibull = clue to multiple competing failure modes (G.1 example: 10 of 19 failures at one coastal base
→ salt-air corporate cause; first Weibull β = 0.75 (infant mortality), coastal Weibull stress-corrosion wear-out
β = 11.9). Notation: p = portion/batch size with distribution F₁; F, F₁, F₂, F₃ = failure distributions; R's
= reliabilities (any distributional shape can be substituted for Fₙ).

**Mixture models:**
- **Competing risk (G.2):** `F = 1 − (1 − F₁)(1 − F₂)` — homogeneous population, whole population at risk from
  both modes; a *series reliability* problem (R = R₁·R₂). Example: ASIC in plastic encapsulation with micro-
  BGA solders — solder-ball crack propagation vs. moisture penetration.
- **Simple mixture (G.3):** `F = p·F₁ + (1 − p)·F₂` — two independent subpopulations, no common failure modes
  (rarely fits exactly; useful approximation for competing-risk mixtures). Example: some solder balls with
  voids.
- **Competing-risk mixture (G.4):** `F = p[1 − (1 − F₁)(1 − F₂)] + (1 − p)F₂` — one subpopulation subject to
  unique mode F₁ *and* common mode F₂ (most real mixtures). Example: tyre wobbling (F₁) vs puncture (F₂).

**Practice (G.1):** preferably separate modes by physical analysis of parts/environment and analyse separately
rather than statistically. Mode 1 failures as suspensions for mode 2 and vice versa (Annex E.4 relay example:
welding vs contact erosion). Splitting purely by plotting position causes errors (some wear-out failures occur
early and some infant-mortality late). Minimum **20 failures** for credible 2-mode mixture; **50+** for other
mixtures. With many modes mixed, doglegs disappear and **β → 1** (≈ assuming exponential; misleading; avoid
whenever categorization is possible). Steep-then-shallow dogleg = batch problem ("perpetual survivors" not
subject to the mode).

Related: steep slopes (β > 4) hide curvature/outliers/doglegs (Annex A.3). Case studies (Annex A): main oil
pump low-time failures traced to oversized parts (batch/machining change); augmenter pump bearings failing
200–400 h with 650 longer-lived items (two dominant modes, β = 0.75 and 11.9; bearing-cage cost-reduction
process change).

**Burn-in / bathtub:** production acceptance tests, burn-in, reliability stress screening (RSS) exist to
remove early failures before delivery (8.1.2; Annex H cites burn-in/ESS as a reason items are aged before
delivered "as zero time" → motivates t0 shifts; see also Jensen & Petersen "Burn-In" in the bibliography).

## 12. Prediction, warranty, comparison, accelerated testing

- **B-lives / L percentiles:** read from plot or computed from the Weibull equation `B_p = η[−ln(1−p)]^{1/β}`;
  B10 special prominence (bearings L10); sudden-death gives L₆…L₃₀ direct.
- **Mission reliability:** `R(t) = exp[−(t/η)^β]` (Eq. 21), with lower confidence limits (10.6); MTTF =
  `η Γ(1+1/β)` (Eq. 19).
- **Warranty/guarantee/contract work:** low-B-percentile estimates and their confidence limits (11.2);
  WeiBayes for zero/one-failure substantiation of redesigns (12.5).
- **Comparing samples:** via confidence intervals, especially for β (10.1 NOTE); compare MRR vs MLE (11.3);
  WeiBayes line vs old-design line (12.5). Formal constant-failure-rate test in IEC 60605-6.
- **Accelerated testing:** *not* covered in the body of this standard; Nelson's "Accelerated Testing" [21] is
  cited in the bibliography only. Time-to-failure data from field or test, or stress as the "age" variable, is
  what the standard supports.

## 13. Other distributions (Clause 14)

Log-normal is the **second choice** for life data (skewed-right x, ln x normal). Applications: flaw sizes, RF
parameters, repair times; progressive deterioration where the rate of change increases with deterioration
(performance loss, crack growth to rupture, vibration amplitude). If the deterioration rate is linear →
Weibull. Physically: log-normal = multiplication of effects; Weibull = linear growth rate. Log-normal first
choice when there is good prior information AND **more than twenty failures**; common for materials properties,
times to repair, crack growth, some semiconductor chip failures. Prior knowledge: the distribution of the
Weibull β is approximately log-normal; η is more normally distributed.
