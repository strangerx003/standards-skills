# EN 50129:2018 — Safety-Related Electronic Systems for Signalling — Full Reference

BS EN 50129:2018 (CENELEC Nov 2018; approved 2018-06-07), "Railway applications — Communication, signalling and processing systems — Safety related electronic systems for signalling". ICS 93.100. Prepared under Mandate M/483; supports Regulation (EU) 402/2013 (CSM-RA) and Directive 2008/57/EC. Dates: DoP 2019-05-23, DoW 2021-11-23. Normative: Clauses 1–8 + Annexes A, B, C, E; informative: Annexes D, F, G, ZZ.

**Supersedes:** EN 50129:2003. **Withdraws:** CLC/TR 50451:2007 (systematic allocation of safety integrity requirements), CLC/TR 50506-1:2007 and CLC/TR 50506-2:2009 (explanatory reports on the former standard).

**Position in the EN 50126 series (Fig. 1):** applies to safety-related electronic systems/subsystems/equipment for railway signalling; EN 50128 covers the **software** of those systems; EN 50159 covers **safety-related communication**; EN 50126-1/-2 provide the **RAMS process and systems approach to safety**. The three railway standards (50126-1/-2, 50128, 50129) together are the railway-sector equivalent of the IEC/EN 61508 series — "when compliance with these documents has been demonstrated, further evaluation of compliance with the EN 61508 series is not required" (Introduction). Scope emphasises **EN 50126-1 life-cycle phases 5–10** (architecture and apportionment → system acceptance); Clause 8 covers the subsequent phases (11–12).

Significant changes vs EN 50129:2003 (Foreword + Annex G): alignment with EN 50126-1:2017 life cycle; Clause 5 = development requirements through phase 9; Clause 8 = safety acceptance and subsequent phases; new Clause 6 (pre-existing items, safety-related tools, physical & IT security, SASC provisions); Safety Case structure in dedicated Clause 7; Annex A aligned with EN 50126-2:2017 (THR/TFFR/SIL); former Annex D **merged into Annex A and made normative**; **Annex E made normative**; new informative Annex F (UPICs/FPGAs); new 5.3.13 (SRACs) and 5.3.15 (ISA); term "SIL 0" abolished (**(A.5.1 Note)** non-safety-related functions are simply "not safety-related"; the least demanding level is **Basic Integrity**).

## 1. Scope and applicability (Clause 1)

- Applicable to safety-related electronic systems, subsystems and equipment for railway signalling, both **generic products/systems** (a class of applications) and **specific applications**.
- Functional safety only — *not* occupational health & safety aspects.
- Applies to **all life-cycle phases**, focusing on **phases 5 (architecture and apportionment) to 10 (system acceptance)** of EN 50126-1:2017.
- **Not applicable** to systems already accepted before the standard existed; applied "so far as reasonably practicable" to modifications/extensions of existing systems.
- General-purpose/COTS equipment procured for use in an SRES: as a minimum, evidence that the equipment is either **not relied on for safety** or **can be relied on for safety-related functions** (→ 6.2).
- Aimed at railway duty holders, suppliers, assessors, safety authorities; does **not** define an approval process for safety authorities.
- Signalling system definition (3.1.51): systems controlling/protecting train movements; applies up to the interface with braking/traction/other train systems on board.

**Normative references:** EN 50124-1 (insulation coordination), EN 50125-1/-3 (environmental conditions), EN 50126-1:2017, EN 50126-2:2017, EN 50128 (software), EN 60664-1 (solid insulation).
**Bibliography (key):** EN 50121 series, EN 50124-2, EN 50155, EN 50159, EN 31010, EN 61025 (FTA), EN 61078 (RBD), EN 61165 (Markov), EN 60812 (FMEA), EN 61508 series (esp. -2, -7), EN 61882 (HAZOP), EN 62502 (ETA), IEC 61709, IEC 62443-3-3, EN ISO 9001, Mü8004 (German Federal Railways principles), UIC/ORE A155 reports, MIL-HDBK-338B, FMD-2013, CLC/TR 50451.

## 2. Key definitions and abbreviations (Clause 3)

**Definitions (verbatim substance, OCR-faithful):**
- ** THR = Tolerable Hazard Rate** — the quantified safety target per hazard, derived by the Risk Assessment; the interface from operational environment to the signalling system is defined by a list of hazards with associated THRs.
- ** TFFR = Tolerable Functional Failure Rate** — tolerable rate of functional (unsafe) failure of a function, per hour and per function; the minimum systematic **and** random safety-integrity target of the involved function (A.4.3.2).
- ** FFR = Functional Failure Rate** — the actual (calculated asymptotic) hazardous functional failure rate.
- ** FR = Failure Rate** of an item; unit h⁻¹ (or distance/cycles where more relevant).
- ** SDT = Safe Down Time = T_d + T_n** — detection time plus negation time: the time required to detect and negate the last fault before the output becomes hazardous with respect to the TFFR (**B.2.1**). **SDR = Safe Down Rate = 1/SDT.** T_d = time between failure and fault detection (test interval for periodic/auto detection; lifetime if no detection provision; mean time between uses if detection via operational use; interval between periodic maintenance tests for stored equipment). T_n = negation time (fault detection → safe state enforced). T_r = repair time; **DT = T_d + T_n + T_r (Down Time)**.
- ** fail-safe (3.1.14):** "able to enter or remain in a safe state in the event of a failure."
- ** safe state (3.1.39):** "condition which continues to preserve safety."
- ** negation (3.1.28):** "enforcement of a safe state following detection of a hazardous fault."
- ** random fault (3.1.34):** "unpredictable occurrence of a fault"; ** systematic fault (3.1.57):** "inherent fault in the specification, design, construction, installation, operation or maintenance of a system, subsystem or equipment."
- ** random failure integrity (3.1.33):** "degree to which a system is free from hazardous random faults"; ** systematic failure integrity (3.1.56):** "degree to a system… free from hazardous systematic faults".
- ** Basic Integrity (3.1.2):** integrity attribute for safety-related functions with a **TFFR higher than (less demanding than) 10⁻⁵ h⁻¹**, or for non-safety-related functions assigned basic-integrity requirements per EN 50126-2.
- ** safety integrity (3.1.45):** "ability of a safety-related system to achieve its required safety functions under all the stated conditions within a stated operational environment and within a stated duration."
- ** safety integrity level (3.1.46):** "one of a number of defined discrete levels for specifying the safety integrity requirements of safety-related functions to be allocated to the safety-related systems."
- ** DC fault model (3.1.10):** stuck-at, stuck-open, open/high-impedance outputs, short circuits between signal lines, and for ICs short circuits between any two connections (pins).
- ** diversity (3.1.9):** two or more different ways/means to achieve an objective — a defence against common-cause failure (physically different, or functional diversity).
- ** common-cause failures (3.1.4):** failures of multiple items, otherwise considered independent, resulting from a single cause.
- ** stress profile (3.1.52):** degree and number of external influences a system can withstand while performing its required functionality.
- ** pre-existing item (3.1.30), cross-acceptance (3.1.7), safety case (3.1.44):** "documented demonstration that the product (e.g. a system, subsystem or equipment) complies with the specified safety requirements"; ** technical safety report (3.1.58)** is part of the safety case; SRAC (3.2, used in 5.3.13) = preconditions passed on to the next user.
- **Independence of roles (3.1.25):** "freedom from involvement in the same intellectual, commercial and/or management entity."
- **Note:** **MTBSF** is *not* a term used in EN 50129 (the operative metrics are THR/TFFR/FFR/SDT); MTBF appears only in the abbreviation list and worked examples.

**Abbreviations (3.2):** CCF, COTS, DES (Designer), DT (Down Time = T_d+T_n+T_r), EMC, EMI, ESD, FFR, FMEA, FR, FTA, HAZOP, HW, GASC, GPSC, ISA, MTBF, PC (Programmable Component), PM, RAMS, SASC, SDR (=1/SDT), SDT (=T_d+T_n), SIL, SW, **SRAC**, TFFR, **THR**, T_d/T_n/T_r, UIC, **UPIC**, VAL, VER.

## 4. Quality management and safety management (Clause 5)

### 4.1 Conditions for acceptance (5.1)
A system is accepted as adequately safe when **all three** are fulfilled and documented in the Safety Case:
1. **Quality management process** (5.2);
2. **Safety management process** (5.3);
3. **Functional and technical safety** — evidence in the Technical Safety Report (7.2), Annexes A/B/C/E.

### 4.2 Quality management (5.2)
- Purpose: "minimize the incidence of human errors and improve process performance at each stage of the life cycle, and thus reduce the risk of systematic faults." Applied to **all** safety-related systems, throughout the EN 50126-1 life cycle (Fig. 3 reproduces the 12-phase V example).
- **Should be compliant with EN ISO 9001.** Typical QMR content (5.2 Note): organizational structure; quality planning/procedures; requirement specification; design control; design verification and reviews; application engineering; procurement and manufacture; product identification/traceability; handling and storage; inspection and testing; non-conformance/corrective action; packaging and delivery; installation and commissioning; operation and maintenance; quality monitoring/feedback; documentation and records; **configuration management/change control**; personnel competency and training; quality audits; decommissioning/disposal.

### 4.3 Safety management elements (5.3)
- **Defence-in-depth intent (5.3.1):** QM reduces systematic faults; the safety management process "aims at minimizing the **residual** risk of safety-related systematic faults and security threats (including IT-Security)". Depth of evidence scaled to Basic Integrity / SIL 1–4. Risk Assessment/Hazard Control (EN 50126-1/-2) are always necessary even to conclude a function is not safety-related.
- **Documentation guidelines (5.3.2):** documentation plan; content checklists; separation of requirements documents from development documentation; grouping by safety life cycle; standardized templates; formal notation for logical functions / natural language for intent / graphical examples with semantic definitions / glossary; facilitation of impact analysis (document breakdown structure); no redundancy; confidentiality classification with storage/access/modification traceability.
- **Safety life cycle (5.3.3, Fig. 4):** top-down design phases mirrored by bottom-up integration/validation ("V"); artefacts: system requirements; hazard analysis & risk assessment; safety requirements (functional + integrity); QMR/SMR; systematic vs random failure integrity evidence; TSR; architecture/HW/SW design ↔ integration & installation tests / HW validation / SW validation.
- **Safety organisation (5.3.4):** competent personnel; documented competence assessment; role records; **independence of roles**: phases 1–4 per EN 50126-2:2017 7.2; **phases 5–10 (5.3.4.2, Fig. 5)**: for **any SIL 1–4 and Basic Integrity** — Designer ≠ Verifier; Designer ≠ Validator; the **Validator** must have autonomy/responsibility for an independent judgement and inform the PM; Validator may also act as Verifier but then another competent person reviews the V&V outputs. **For SIL 3 and SIL 4 additionally: the Validator shall not report to the Project Manager.** ISA never in the project team, never reporting to the PM (5.3.15).
- **Safety plan (5.3.5):** per EN 50126-1 7.3.2.3; structure of the safety management organisation, activities, milestones, review requirements; updated on any alteration; covers HW and SW (EN 50128 referenced); includes a **Safety Case Plan** (scope, structure, underlying cases, responsibilities, timescale) and verification/validation planning.
- **Hazard log (5.3.6):** hazards + risk classification + risk control measures; updated on change.
- **Safety requirements specification (5.3.7):** derived via hazard identification/analysis, risk assessment, SIL allocation; separate document or part of the functional SRS; Table E.2 gives per-SIL guidance; classification per A.2.
- **System design for safety (5.3.8):** top-down structured methodology with controlled documentation; strict HW/SW relationship management (SW requirements spec, HW/SW integration spec) with **EN 50128 adherence**; targets for tolerable failure rates per selected architecture (Annex A); design-for-maintainability (structured design, verified interfaces, modularity, obsolescence preparation). Minimum design documentation: architecture description, safety design principles, interfaces, modification procedures, manufacturing documentation, application documentation.
- **O&M plan (5.3.9):** operational conditions for safe monitoring; safety-performance monitoring vs prediction; failure-trend and hazardous-failure report analysis; incident/accident investigations delta to the design assumptions.
- **Safety verification (5.3.10):** per-phase checks — correctness/adequacy of hazard analyses; deliverable-vs-prior-phase compliance; compliance with phase requirements; adequacy of methods/tools/techniques; test cases/executed tests. Hazard analyses, test plans/specs **written by or reviewed/agreed by the verifier**. Test reports record: responsibilities, versions of spec/elements/config data/tools, results with discrepancies. Table E.8 guidance.
- **Safety validation (5.3.11):** objectives — safety requirements specified, implemented, verified; system fulfils the safety target and is fit for intended use. Validation strategy justification (manual/automated, static/dynamic, analytical/statistical, real/simulated). Validator writes/agrees analyses and tests; audits that manufacture, installation, maintenance, operation do not compromise design concepts; may also act as verifier (output cross-reviewed). Validation report: versions, tools, simulation models; traceability completeness of requirements; deviations and resulting SRACs; **fitness-for-purpose conclusion**. Table E.8 guidance.
- **Safety qualification tests (5.3.12):** purpose — confidence in operational requirements, reliability/safety targets, and to allow **operation before final safety approval** subject to precautions and monitoring (this is the standard's "probationary/provisional operation" provision — *the term "probationary" itself is not used; the mechanism is provisional safety approval via qualification testing*). Extent/duration agreed duty holder ↔ supplier, justified by novelty/complexity; record of conditions/restrictions before testing; completed before operation with full safety responsibility; a statement follows recording operational conditions and the authorisation level (provisional or final); account documented in **TSR §6**.
- **SRAC management (5.3.13, Table 1 template):** SRACs = assumptions/conditions to be satisfied when integrating the item into a wider system; each SRAC **linked to at least one hazard**; must be uniquely identifiable, receiver-addressed, self-evident, complete, auditable, accessible (no unresolvable references), consistent in all languages. Avoid: disguised SRACs (characteristics), design-avoidable ones, incomplete/impossible/ambiguous ones, "erroneously considered as applied". SRACs imported from related safety cases must be **closures with evidence or re-exports**. No SRAC needed where a general legal/normative rule already covers the hazard. Template fields: Identifier, Title, Origin, Hazard(s), Receiver, Text, Verification (example compliance method).
- **Safety justification (5.3.14):** the Safety Case (Clause 7).
- **Independent safety assessment (5.3.15):** assesses (1) compliance with this standard, (2) adequacy of the safety process, (3) fitness for purpose safety-wise. Not restricted to document review; methods may include interviews, analyses, audits, test witnessing, physical inspections; may be more than one ISA; accepted by the duty holder within the legal framework; shall notify concerns outside its remit. **For Basic Integrity the ISA only evaluates process conformity up to the SIL allocation (phases 4 and 5).** Results in an ISA report, which may add operating conditions.

## 5. Elements with independent life cycles (Clause 6)

### 5.1 Pre-existing items (6.2)
- Scope: **complex** items (no complete deterministic functional model possible) **not** developed per this standard or its previous versions — e.g. monitors, modems/switches/routers, operating systems, boards, industrial/personal computers. Non-complex HW components → Annex C regime only. Items compliant *with a previous version of this standard* are accepted **without additional demonstration**.
- Typical deficiencies: missing process evidence (QM/SM not in a structured safety case; freedom from systematic faults not demonstrated) and missing technical evidence (unknown properties, no embedded fault-tolerance/fault management).
- **Complete pre-existing systems** built under other safety standards (6.2.2): trace existing evidence; close gaps per this standard; **for SIL 1–4 an independent safety assessment shall guarantee compliance**.
- **Pre-existing equipment performing part of a safety function, incl. COTS (6.2.3):** minimum information set — functionality; interfaces; HW/SW constraints; **failure rate**; environmental and conditions-of-use data. Item identified, under configuration control. Hazardous functional failure modes identified (interface hazard analysis; full FMEA often infeasible). For each hazardous mode at the boundary requiring Basic Integrity–SIL 4: either (4.1) demonstrate it **cannot credibly occur** (internal architecture, data structure, inherent physical properties), or (4.2) provide a **complete safety demonstration per the required SIL**, or (4.3) **externally negate** it with a safe state enforced within the required time (analogy to B.3.1). Where contributors cannot be located inside the equipment, use the **full failure rate of the equipment** and demonstrate compatibility with the function's TFFR. Integration as a **black box** for design/V&V/assessment. A change-management strategy for all SILs (e.g. disable automatic software updates).

### 5.2 Safety-related tools (6.3)
- **Directly affecting** tools: outputs contribute to design/implementation of safety functions, errors could directly create a systematic hazardous fault — e.g. translation/generation tools, signalling design tools (block sectioning, safety distances, control tables), circuit parameter tools, data/parameter tools, some modelling/formal checkers in IDEs.
- **Indirectly affecting:** test/verification tools, diagnostic/monitoring tools, configuration control, requirement tracing, safety-case editors, measuring instruments, office software. SW support tools → EN 50128.
- Tools should cooperate (output format feeds the next tool), be available over the system lifetime. For directly-affecting tools: hazard analysis of tool + process; generic validation confirms correct application; then **one of**:
  (a) **verification of tool output** — tests/checks/analyses with coverage of functions and domains consistent with the target SIL, capable of detecting the relevant error classes;
  (b) **tool proven in use** — periodic confirmation by analysis/examination with objective evidence; qualitative history (projects, anomaly lists, successive versions) shows output trustworthiness;
  (c) **tool proven by analysis and testing** — documented: version + manual, tool functions analysed, test equipment, cases/results, discrepancies, conclusions/use constraints;
  (d) **tool diversity** — two diverse tools with output comparison; second tool regenerates the input for comparison; independent principle-based rule-checker of the first tool's output;
  (e) **tool built to an appropriate SIL**.

### 5.3 Physical security and IT-security (6.4)
- Two threat classes from unauthorized access: **physical** (established good practice: prevent direct access) and **IT-security** (logical access; modern communication concepts; e.g. remote manipulation affecting functional safety).
- "There is no inherent relationship between security and safety requirements"; SIL concept **not** applicable to IT-security requirements; probabilistic evaluation of IT threats considered **infeasible** (treated like systematic faults). The standard does not specify security development/operation requirements — ISO 27000 series, ISO/IEC/TR 19791 and the **IEC 62443** series apply; EN 50159 covers some communication-security aspects.
- IT-security threats **shall be managed within Risk Assessment and Hazard Control** (or referenced analyses) when an impact on functional safety is reasonably foreseeable and not excludable by simple arguments (e.g. no connection to untrusted networks). Measures recorded/referenced in **TSR §4.5**. Safety-vs-security tension note: fast security updates of SW vs thoroughly V&V'd, approved safety-related SW.

## 6. Safety requirements derivation — THR → TFFR → SIL (Annex A)

### 6.1 Safety requirements taxonomy (A.2, Fig. A.1)
System requirements split non-safety vs **safety requirements**; the latter split **functional safety requirements** (which *include* safety integrity requirements) vs other safety (occupational, fire). **Safety integrity (A.3)** = **systematic failure integrity** (non-quantifiable; caused by human errors in specification, design, manufacture, installation, operation, maintenance, modification; achieved by QM + SM + technical defences) + **random failure integrity** (quantifiable via probabilistic calculation on component failure rates/modes and disclosure times; for components with inherent physical properties a **hazardous failure rate of zero** is generally assumed). **Both must be satisfied**; residual hazard risk remains after probabilistic assessment, hence qualitative SIL-level measures are also required.

### 6.2 Global process (A.4.1, Fig. A.2)
- Well-defined interface between operational environment and signalling system defines responsibilities (duty holder ↔ supplier): **list of hazards + associated THR**.
- Risk Assessment (duty holder) → THRs → **Hazard Control** (supplier or joint): safety assumptions/functions; architecture with allocated functions; **hazard analysis (causal analysis)**; SIL determination; SRS; compliance analysis; identification of **new hazards from design** (A.4.4 — declared to the duty holder; if relatable to an existing hazard, check the combined rate stays under the THR; otherwise duty holder decides relevance and owns mitigation; full record in hazard log and safety case); reliability requirements. Both Risk Assessment and Hazard Control undergo ISA. Iterative loop.

### 6.3 Risk assessment details (A.4.2)
- System definition: functions/interfaces/O&M context; generic developments must convert context assumptions into **SRACs**.
- Risk analysis = hazard identification (systematic: **empirical phase** — checklists/past experience + **deductive phase** — structured what-if, HAZOP, FMEA) + consequence analysis. Warning: huge trivial hazard lists signal a poorly conducted study. Hazards at the *system boundary* are hierarchically re-instantiated as subsystem-level hazards (Fig. A.3); hazards ordered by perceived risk; all recorded in the **hazard log**.
- **Risk evaluation = derivation of THRs** per selected **risk acceptance criteria** (not defined by this standard — national/international legislation; see EN 50126-2). Methods: explicit individual-risk estimation; comparison with existing systems/acknowledged rules of technology (statistical/analytical); or **qualitative approaches provided they produce hazards + corresponding THRs**. Duty holders are free to set THRs at any level (general system-level targets or per-function targets).

### 6.4 Hazard control & apportionment machinery (A.4.3)
- Core two-stage causal analysis (A.4.3.2/.3, Fig. A.4):
  - **Stage 1 — THR → TFFR and SIL.** The hazard is associated with a functional failure of a function or set of functions; the THR then determines the **TFFR(s)**; since causes may be systematic and random, TFFR is the **minimum systematic and random integrity target** of the function; the **SIL (or Basic Integrity) is set from the TFFR using the SIL table (Table A.1)** — *at the latest at the level of the last independent functions* (functions each capable of counteracting the hazard regardless of the others, e.g. level-crossing barriers vs lights; not cooperating functions like a braking-curve calculation and the braking command). One-to-one hazard-function relation ⇒ **THR = TFFR**.
  - Combination-logic apportionment by any suitable method: RBD, FTA, BDD, Markov (care when independence is claimed).
  - **Stage 2 — TFFR → hazardous failure rates (FR) of physical elements**, with **SIL inherited unchanged** ("the SIL adopted by EN 50128 for the safety-related software is the same as the function SIL"; EN 50128 **SIL 0 satisfies Basic Integrity** software requirements). Example: a signal protecting route occupancy depends on several track circuits, each a physically independent component with hazardous failure rate < TFFR.
- **Independence among functions (A.4.3.4).** For AND-combinations (independent functions jointly preventing a hazard): freedom from common random **and** systematic causes shown by **CCF analysis**; each function alone must prevent the hazard; functions must be **diverse**; each function's integrity must not rely on the other's outputs (a function providing input to another is a **common cause** — corresponds to an OR gate with additive TFFRs, e.g. interlocking track-circuit states feeding a RBC/ATP movement authority); faults in any function detected and a safe state enforced in time compatible with the higher-level THR/TFFR, **by a detection means independent of the function**, with detection coverage sufficient for the higher-level target. Fig. A.5: any two functions can be made independent by separating their common causes from the residual functions in the FTA.
- **Composite fail-safe architectures with identical redundancy:** common systematic failures are unavoidable ⇒ **TFFR is NOT apportioned further**; the AND is used **only for FR apportionment** (random-independence only) — SIL inherited.
- **Independence from common random causes (A.4.3.5):** AND faults in FT calculations are meaningful only with justified independence — **a CCF analysis is necessary to claim independence from common random causes** (physical influences: galvanic connections, EMC, etc.; also systematic event couplings like power-supply switchover inrush — covered as "random effects" by physical common-cause analysis). Preconditions for AND usage in apportionment: implementations independent from common random causes; **safe down times (T_d+T_n) defined/estimated and achieved**.
- **Independence from common systematic causes (A.4.3.6):** diversity of human resources and life-cycle processes contributes to integrity; higher SIL ⇒ higher process/HR independence. When THR/TFFR is split by two independent functions, the **surrounding life-cycle phases are managed at the SIL of the first-level hazard/function** (system definition, risk analysis, requirement specification, architecture/apportionment before; integration, system validation after); development teams need sufficient organisational/personal independence (SW: EN 50128).

### 6.5 SIL levels and achievement (A.5.1)
- SIL 1…SIL 4 or Basic Integrity; **SIL 4 highest, Basic Integrity lowest**; "SIL 0" no longer used. SIL addresses the *qualitative* appreciation of QM, SM and technical safety conditions.
- Suppliers may start generic developments bottom-up and obtain a GPSC approval without a downstream risk analysis — but on deployment the required THRs from the risk analysis shall be fulfilled.
- **Achieving a SIL requires ALL of (Fig. A.6):** quality-management conditions + safety-management conditions + technical safety conditions + **quantified safety target (TFFR)**. Meeting the target alone ≠ meeting the SIL, and vice versa.
- Having followed the SIL's required measures/methods, **systematic failures need not be reconsidered when demonstrating TFFR achievement** (double-counting avoided).

### 6.6 Table A.1 — TFFR ↔ SIL (normative)
| TFFR per hour and per function | SIL |
|---|---|
| 10⁻⁹ ≤ TFFR < 10⁻⁸ | **4** |
| 10⁻⁸ ≤ TFFR < 10⁻⁷ | **3** |
| 10⁻⁷ ≤ TFFR < 10⁻⁶ | **2** |
| 10⁻⁶ ≤ TFFR < 10⁻⁵ | **1** |
| TFFR > 10⁻⁵ | **Basic Integrity** (5.3.4.2, 5.3.15, 6.2.3, 7.2 + EN 50126-2) |

*(OCR renders exponents unreliably; the band edges above are the standard's values and agree with the Basic Integrity definition "TFFR > 10⁻⁵ h⁻¹" in 3.1.2 and the 10⁻⁹ extreme-frequency rule below.)*

- **Note A.1 (mode):** only **one frequency column** — no demand-mode/probability-on-demand column (explicitly unlike "many other standards"): fewer ambiguities; **all demand-mode systems can be modelled as continuous-mode systems**; continuous control-and-command systems dominate modern signalling. ⇒ There is **no "probability of dangerous undetected failure per demand" column** and **no SFF/PFD-style column** anywhere in EN 50129.
- **TFFR below 10⁻⁹ h⁻¹:** either apportion to **functionally independent sub-functions** (SIL per sub-function), or implement **at least SIL 4 measures** and combine with other technical/operational measures to reach the target.
- Upper end (≥10⁻⁵): Basic Integrity obligations — 5.3.4.2 (independence/organisation), 5.3.15 (ISA up to SIL allocation), 6.2.3 (pre-existing equipment treatment), 7.2 (reduced TSR) and EN 50126-2.

## 7. Risk analysis and CCA recap (cross-view)

- Frequency-oriented mitigation: Hazard Control typically reduces risk by reducing **occurrence frequencies**, not consequences (A.4.2 Note).
- **CCF/CCA embedded in the risk analysis at three points:** (1) A.4.3.4 — CCF analysis proves AND-gates between functions (both random and systematic common causes); (2) A.4.3.5 — CCF analysis is mandatory to justify *any* random-cause independence claim; (3) B.3.5.1 — CCF analysis assures a hazardous multiple fault is a random combination only. CCF factoring technique: Fig. A.5 (split functions into CCF-affected and independent sub-functions in the FT).
- **Randomness handling:** probabilistic calculation for random failure integrity only (constant-rate, exponential assumptions; field data preferred; pessimistic full-rate assumptions for unanalysed hazardous IC modes; zero hazardous rate legitimately only for justified inherent fail-safety per Annex C).

## 8. Annex B — Management of faults (normative), incl. the quantitative formulas

### 8.1 Timing model (B.2.1, Fig. B.1)
failure → **T_d** (detection time) → **T_n** (negation time, safe state enforced) = **SDT**; then repair/logistics **T_r**; total **DT**. T_d = test interval (automatic or periodic testing) or maintenance interval (staff detection) or lifetime (no provision) or mean time between uses (operational use) or interval between maintenance tests (stored equipment). **Composite** fail-safety: system remains safe but an additional failure during undetected/negable period is hazardous (Fig. B.4); **reactive** fail-safety: transient *permissive* output bounded by SDT (Fig. B.5). In a safety context the reproduction time may be negligible because control measures keep an enforced safe state; for low-SIL components negation may occur by restoration (SDT = DT).

### 8.2 Composition of items (B.2.2)
- **Eq (B.1)** — two independent restorable items A and B (2oo2-active derivation per IEC 61165 Fig. B.7; using SDR instead of DT is an upper bound):
  `FFR₀₀ = (FR_A × FR_B)/(SDR_A × SDR_B) × (SDR_A + SDR_B)`
- **Eq (B.2)** — identical items:
  `FFR = 2·FR²/SDR = 2·FR²·SDT = 2·FR²·(T_d + T_n)`
- Validity assumptions to justify per application: constant rates over time; faults hazardous in combination detected **and negated**; hazardous CCF negligible; **FR × SDT ≪ 1**.
- Worked examples: FR = 10⁻⁴/h (MTBF 10 000 h), SDT = 1 h ⇒ FFR = 2×10⁻⁸/h. SDT = 1 000 h (maintenance-detected) ⇒ ~10⁻⁵/h (only 10× better than single-item MTBF rate) — and FR·SDT = 0.1 so the approximation is poor; lifetime SDT loses nearly all gain.

### 8.3 Single faults — fail-safety (B.3.1, Fig. B.2)
Rule: any safety-related function shall meet its TFFR in the event of a random fault; **SIL 3/4 must remain safe under any kind of recognised single hazardous random HW fault** (negligible-effect faults may be ignored). Three fail-safety techniques:
1. **Composite fail-safety** — ≥2 independent items per safety function; non-restrictive activity only on the necessary agreement; a hazardous fault detected and negated in time vs TFFR before a second fault; dormant faults caught by online periodic monitoring.
2. **Reactive fail-safety** — single functional item + independent checking/testing/detection function ("second item") with rapid negation (encoding, multiple computation and comparison, continual testing).
3. **Inherent fail-safety** — single item, all credible failure modes non-hazardous; per-Annex C identification; effects demonstrated non-hazardous via physical tests/technical justifications/simulation; credible modes justified with hazardous rate ≈ 0; also usable for sub-functions (independence enforcement, shut-down).
Fig. B.2 decision flow: single-fault hazard? → M-out-of-N redundancy → second-fault hazard? → composite (B.3.3.1/B.3.3.2) vs reactive (B.3.3.3 for "2nd fault not hazardous" branch, and mitigation-by-test rapid reaction) vs inherent (Annex C.4). Demonstration by **structured analysis**: top-down **FTA** supported where necessary by bottom-up **FMEA** (Table E.6); qualitative + quantitative where data exist; failure-rate mode-split justified; field-data-based component rates.

### 8.4 Independence of items (B.3.2, Fig. B.3)
Influence classes: **A** physical internal, **B** functional internal (via transmitted information/data/signals — case-by-case protective measures), **C** physical external (EMI/ESD, climatic/mechanical/chemical stress, power supply, external I/O voltages), **D** functional external.
- **B.3.2.2 (SIL 3/4), primary vs secondary independence:** **primary** independence between main items whose simultaneous malfunction is hazardous; **secondary** independence between a main item and an "additional (checker) item" may be less stringent (a simultaneous main+additional malfunction is not hazardous provided main-item faults are negated before becoming hazardous via another main item; **N-out-of-M** = M main items with additional items).
  Primary: no non-intentional galvanic connections (insulation at least **reinforced insulation per EN 50124-1**: PCB same-layer/different-layer, insulated wires in a cable, transformer windings and opto-couplers — max internal temperatures limited incl. fault conditions to avoid carbonisation); interfaces protected by devices with **inherent fail-safe properties**; cross-talk control (different supplies per item or sufficiently low ground impedance even under faults; separation distances demonstrated by calculation/measurement; additional shielding/doubling under faults).
  Secondary: insulation at least **basic insulation (EN 50124-1)**; protecting devices need not have inherent fail-safe properties; **voltage monitoring recommended** and it should not be influenced by the monitored (main) item's faults.
- **B.3.2.3 (SIL 3/4), external influences (Type C):** EMI/ESD per **EN 50121-4**; climatic conditions normally per spec with measures against out-of-spec operation; mechanical stress measures per **EN 50125-1/-3**; chemical stress measures; supply-voltage protection — non-permitted voltages disclosed by voltage monitoring triggering an enforced safe state before hazards; monitoring correct over the whole life cycle, **redundant if its own faults are not disclosable**; external I/O protection — worst-case voltages (process + EMI-induced), clearances per surge requirements of EN 50124-1, creepage at least per EN 50124-1 at max rated rms voltage, **larger of clearance/creepage decisive**.
- **B.3.2.4 (SIL 1/2 exceptions):** same recommendations may be relaxed provided failures stay within the TFFR — **basic** insulation permissible; protectors without inherent fail-safe properties permissible; voltage monitoring may be omitted.

### 8.5 Detection of single faults (B.3.3)
- General (B.3.3.1): first fault (alone or in combination) detected and negated — safe state enforced — within the quantified target; demonstrated by **FMEA + quantified FTA** (Table E.6). Composite: risk of a second fault occurring during **detection+negation (SDT)** below the probabilistic target. Reactive: maximum total detection+negation time ≤ specified transient-hazard duration limit. Techniques and supporting calculations shown; failure-rate data sources identified. SIL 3/4: single hazardous faults **should** be negated by **technical means** (not merely procedures).
- **B.3.3.2 — composite 2oo2 provisions:** each item X, Y needs **independent detection/negation** (may be performed by the other item; otherwise lifetime/periodic-test interval counts). Periodic tests representative of all credible faults, completed **< SDT**; fault coverage of **integrated circuits** per **Table B.1**; faults not covered by periodic tests must be demonstrated not hazardous alone or in combination.
  - **Eq (B.3):** for two items with equal SDT: `SDT ≤ TFFR / (2·FR_A·FR_B)`
  - With T_d ≫ T_n: SDT ≈ T_d; with a periodic test of interval T₁, T_d ≈ T₁/2, hence **FFR = (FR_A×FR_B)·T₁** and dimensioning **Eq (B.5):** `T₁ ≤ TFFR / (FR_A·FR_B)`
  - **Eq (B.6) — German Mü8004 double-fault rule:** `SDT ≤ k / (1000·(FR_A + FR_B))` with **k = 1 for a 2-out-of-2 system** and **k = 0.5 for a 2-out-of-3 system**; for FR_A = FR_B = FR both rules coincide when TFFR/FR = k/1000; typically **Mü8004 is more conservative** than (B.3).
  - Failure rates taken as a function of the **stress profile** (application/environment); simplified profile allowed if conservative. If not all item-pair combinations are hazardous, detection times per combination, shortest applies. **Power-off allowance:** service interruption up to **400 × permissible fault-detection time**; at restart run all periodic tests or thoroughly test off-line. Note: unpowered electronics reliability ≈ **20× better** (basis for testing-time evaluation).
- **B.3.3.3 — reactive fail-safety (SIL 3/4):** SDT = maximum total detection+reaction time for all single faults affecting safe operation ≤ the specified hazardous-condition limit (example: **SDT < 100 ms** if the controlled equipment is a signalling relay with large rise time); within SDT the first safety failure triggers a safety reaction; the checker/detection function integrity guaranteed throughout life — its loss leads to the safe state.

### 8.6 Retention of safe state (B.3.4, Figs. B.4/B.5)
SIL 3/4: after first-fault detection enter or continue a generally more restrictive safe state within the target time; the safe state shall not be cancelled out by further faults; cancellation only by a controlled corrective procedure; system remains in the safe state for the **permissible delay-times-to-repair**, which must be short enough to meet the target. Composite: first-fault probability × second-fault probability during SDT < target (detection typically cross-channel). Reactive: SDT ≤ transient-limit.

### 8.7 Multiple faults (B.3.5)
- General (B.3.5.1): hazardous multiple faults (double/triple, directly or in combination) detected and negated to the target; demonstration **e.g. by FTA** (Table E.6); **a CCF analysis shall be carried out** to show a hazardous multiple fault can only be a combination of random single faults, never a common-cause fault.
- **B.3.5.2 — composite fail-safety, ≥3 items (3-out-of-N and higher):**
  - if timely detection+negation of a single-item fault is impossible/unsuitable, account for the chance of further faults;
  - **simultaneous faults in two items shall be non-hazardous ⇒ at least three independent items**;
  - periodic tests complete < SDT, representative of credible faults; **IC fault detection per Table B.1**; periodic tests may be limited to dangerous faults of the **DC fault model**;
  - **Eq (B.8):** for double faults in ≥3 identical items A, B, C: `SDT ≤ 2 / (FR_A + FR_B + FR_C)` (engineering rule Mü8004-derived; conservative vs analytical 3-fault formulas where B.1 does not apply);
  - **Eq (B.9):** measures for detection of **triple faults** beyond operational data flow and maintenance tests are **not required if FR ≤ 2×10⁻⁶ h⁻¹ per item**;
  - stress-profile-based FRs as above; per-combination detection times, shortest applies.

### 8.8 Table B.1 — periodic online test measures for IC faults (normative)
| Component | Failure modes (assumed) | Measures (one or combination, justified) |
|---|---|---|
| CPU: register, internal RAM | DC fault model for data & addresses; dynamic crossover of memory cells; abrupt change of information incl. **soft errors**; no/wrong/multiple addressing | **HW comparator** between independent processing units (externally tested or inherently fail-safe) — first-fault detection; or **software reciprocal comparison** (results, intermediate results, test data) triggering safe state |
| Programme counter, stack pointer | DC fault model; address-range change (incl. soft errors) | **HW majority voter** (negates faulty channel; itself externally tested or inherently fail-safe); or **coded processing** in a single item (negation external); other measures with demonstrated coverage |
| Instruction decoding/execution | No definite failure assumption | as above |
| Efficiency caveat | — | comparison-technique efficiency depends on extent of data/signals compared vs faults to detect |
| Clock | Incorrect frequency; period jitter | clock signal/result comparison between channels of an M-out-of-N architecture; or **time monitoring by an independent item (watchdog)** (externally tested or inherently fail-safe) |
| Reset | DC fault model; drift/oscillation; components not initialised to reset | **independent reset generators per unit** + wrong-reset detection by comparison |
| Memory — invariable | All faults affecting data | **CRC signature ≥ 2 words (≥16-bit)**; or **block replication** (parallel second copy, one stored inverted, output compared) |
| Memory — variable | DC fault model (data/addresses); cell crossover; soft errors; no/wrong/multiple addressing | combination from: **RAM test "galpat" / "transparent galpat"**; **RAM test "Abraham"**; **double RAM + HW/SW comparison + read/write test**; **EDC/ECC codes** (see EN 61508-7:2010 A.5); other demonstrated measures |
| IC power supply | DC fault model; drift/oscillation | per-channel **independent supplies** + wrong-supply detection by comparison; **additional voltage monitoring** where not independent or for multiple faults |

**Notes:** soft errors = temporary unintended alteration of stored data/processing from α-particles (package), neutrons, external EMI, internal cross-talk. EDC/ECC/MODIFIED-Hamming effectiveness depends on including the **address** in the code and covering multi-bit common-cause faults (multiple row select, multiple bit-line switches, repaired row/column redundancy) — coverage must be analysed and extra measures taken if needed.

### 8.9 Defence against systematic faults (B.3.6)
Beyond QM/SM (5.2/5.3), **technical measures shall be taken** so a hazardous systematic fault is, as far as reasonably practicable, prevented from creating unacceptable risk — e.g. overall-system architecture configured so that even a hazardous failure of a designed-safe subsystem still makes an accident unlikely.

## 10. Common quantified HW safety integrity & verification practice (THR/TFFR→FR, FTA)

- The TFFR (function level) is the probabilistic safety target (per hour, **the "dangerous functional failure rate" tolerance**); EN 50129 does **not** use "probability of dangerous undetected failure per hour"-style separate tables nor SFF — the Table A.1 banding *is* the mapping, and the quantified safety target verification is by FTA/Markov apportionment with the B-annex formulas.
- Component hazardous rates distributed via **hazardous-failure-rate apportionment** at the physical level with **SIL inheritance** (A.4.3.3); where AND-gated channels, the "per-channel unsafe failure rate" distribution is done only on the **FR** (not TFFR) under random-only independence (A.4.3.4 last paragraph; Fig. D.1).
- Failure-rate inputs scaled by the **stress profile/mission profile** (B.3.3.2, B.3.5.2; application/environment dependent, conservative simplified profiles allowed); **exponential/constant-rate assumptions declared** (B.2.2); optimistic AND results invalid when FR·SDT ≫ small.
- Safety-target verification method: top-down **FTA** (checked against THR/TFFR) supported by bottom-up FMEA; qualitative + quantified with justified field data; detection coverage claims (fault-detection mechanisms with required coverage) tracked into the FT (A.4.3.4 last bullet); **CCF analysis** gate on every AND gate; periodic-test intervals derived per (B.5); architectures with maintenance detection handled via SDT limits.

## 11. Annex D (informative) — Example of THR/TFFR/FR apportionment & SIL allocation (Fig. D.1)

- Hierarchy example: **THR** at hazard level → three functions A, B, C each able to cause the hazard (OR) ⇒ TFFR per function more demanding than THR → **SIL allocated**; downstream: subfunction X (composite fail-safe, random-only independence ⇒ TFFR further apportioned to FR_A/FR_B via AND, **SIL inherited**); equipment function B1 as composite fail-safe (random-only independence, A.4.3.5 requirements); equipment functions C1/C2 demonstrably independent **both systematic and random** (A.4.3.4/.5/.6) ⇒ further TFFR apportionment and **lower SILs may be allocated**; equipment function C3 implementing fail-safe comparison of C1+C2 and their common functionalities **inherits the SIL of Function C** (not needed if two fully independent equipments e.g. both fire the brake).
- Rule recap: AND with full independentness ⇒ apportion TFFR + possibly lower SIL; AND with random-only independence ⇒ apportion FR only, keep SIL.

*Note:* EN 50129 does not use IEC 61508-style **"white-box"/class M or "S-codes"** categorical tables; Annex E's HR/R/NR tables **are** the system-level "measures against systematic failures" scheme, complementary to EN 50128's software technique tables.

## 13. Verification, validation, testing and qualification (system-level view of 5.3.10–5.3.12 + 8.1)

- **V&V (Table E.8):** simulation, formal verification (SIL 3/4 only), functional testing (HR for all SILs, with **representative configuration data**), functional testing **under environmental conditions** per EN 50125-3, design audit/review, documentation inspection, and optional **statistical confidence by use** (proven-in-use criteria above).
- **Test types required at acceptance (8.1(d))** for generic products: **type testing including environmental stress screening** and inspection/test of RAMS-related failure modes, plus overall correct-functional-operation testing.
- **Environmental testing** is anchored in TSR §4 (climatic EN 50125-3, mechanical, altitude ≤ ~1800 m, electrical per EN 50121-3/-4/EN 50124-1/EN 50155, storage and transportation considered) and in Table E.8 row 4.
- **Ageing/wear-out aspects:** no dedicated ageing clause; handled through — (i) component-operation-within-ratings and margins (C.5); (ii) design for maintainability and **obsolescence/technology-evolution preparation** (5.3.8 Note); (iii) temperature/lifetime caveats in Annex C (carbonisation prevention); (iv) periodic online testing across life; (v) performance monitoring in service (5.3.9). **MTTF/MTTR are not defined terms in EN 50129** (MTBF appears only in examples); the safety-side quantities are FR/FFR/TFFR/SDT; safeness is by architecture (composite/reactive/inherent fail-safety) and safe/unsafe failure split is implicit via the DC fault model vs dangerous-fault coverage of Table B.1.

## 14. Hardware safety integrity — UPICs and programmable logic (Annex F, informative)

- **Definitions:** UPIC = FPGA, PLD, EPLD, CPLD, PAL, PLA, LCA — HW items configured via a hardware description language. Two constituents: **UPIC hardware** (treated like non-programmable HW — Annexes B/C/E; embedded hard cores like C.3 ICs) and **UPIC configuration** (logic components/connectivity defined in HDL; **outside EN 50128**; SW running on soft cores → EN 50128; IP cores often reused).
- **Life cycle (F.2, Figs. F.3/F.4, Table F.1):** top-down (Requirements → Architecture & Design → Logic Component Design/Coding/Verification → Physical Implementation → Integration → Validation) or hybrid top-down/bottom-up re-using **pre-existing logic components (IP cores)** — the latter follow **6.2** requirements and substitute the Design/Coding/Testing phases. Documentation set (Table F.1): requirements spec + verification report; architecture/design spec + requirements & integration test spec + verification report; logic component design spec + test spec + verification report; source code + verification; test report + source-code verification report; synthesis/place-and-route/programming files + verification; integration test report; validation report. Mixed-SIL logic components: all treated at the **highest** SIL unless independence demonstrated (recorded in the UPIC architecture). Roles per 5.3.4.2.
- **Architecture & random-fault protection (F.3.1/F.3.2):** SIL allocated at higher level (system/subsystem/equipment hazard analysis), typically including common-mode protections and **self-tests with a maximum allowed execution period per the safety target**. **SIL 3/4 UPIC functions** must remain safe under any recognised single random HW fault ⇒ fail-safety by **composite** or **reactive** structures (as for non-programmable HW; **inherent fail-safety is deemed NOT applicable** because UPIC failure modes are unknown). **SIL 1/2**: a subset of hazardous single faults tolerable within the quantitative target; product-defined measures. **Externally monitored** UPICs (all failures detectable by external elements) may be treated as non-safety-related — but "processing of safety functions not controlled by an external monitoring function is to be avoided".
- **Systematic-fault T&M (F.3.3):** combination of techniques documented; at least one **alternative/equivalent** of each numbered set applied; consistency and phase-coverage considerations; decisions affect only systematic-fault control, not the random-fault architecture. **Simple vs complex UPIC (Fig. F.5):** *simple* only if a comprehensive test+analysis combination ensures full validation with all internal states observable (e.g. a linear feedback shift register); everything else **complex** (typically the case in modern safety applications).
  - **Table F.2 (simple UPIC):** exhaustive functional and black-box tests **HR/HR (SIL 1/2, 3/4)**; identification and analysis of all UPIC failure modes **R/HR**.
  - **Table F.3 — Design & Verification (pre-synthesis; SIL 1/2/3/4):** 1 structured description **HR HR HR HR** (requirements/architecture/design); 2 **design description in HDL HR HR HR HR** (coding); 3 **schematic entry – – NR NR**; 4 widely used design environment **HR HR HR HR**; 5 functional test on component level (HDL test benches) **HR HR HR HR**; 6 restricted use of asynchronous constructs **HR HR HR HR**; 7 design for testability R R R R; 8 modularisation R R HR HR; 9 coverage of verification scenarios (test benches) R R HR HR; 10 **observation of coding guidelines HR HR HR HR**; 11 documentation of simulation results HR×4; 12a code inspection / 12b walk-through R R HR HR.
  - **Table F.4 — Synthesis:** 13 internal consistency checks HR×4; 14 check of IC vendor requirements/constraints HR×4; 15 documentation of synthesis constraints/results/tools HR×4; 16 widely used synthesis tools HR×4; 17 widely used libraries/technologies HR×4; 18 script-based procedure R R HR HR.
  - **Table F.5 — Place & route/layout:** 19 **static timing analysis of critical paths** HR×4; 20a **verification of the gate netlist against a reference model by simulation** HR×4; 20b **comparison of the gate netlist with the reference model (formal equivalence check)** HR×4; 21 design rule check HR×4; 22 widely used design environment HR×4.
  - Descriptions (Tables F.6–F.8): synchronous design with limited clocks, master SET/RESET synchronised via ≥2 flip-flops; avoidance of combinatorial-depth explosion for testability; regression tests automated against reference models; STA coverage conditions (proper timing constraints, fully synchronous, both gate & wire delays); formal equivalence requires complete equivalence proof.
  - Notes: hard cores in FPGAs/CPLDs → Annex C.3 treatment; **high-level synthesis** (C/C++ → HDL) → treated as SW coding per **EN 50128**, with support-tool requirements applying to the synthesis step (like compilation).

## 15. Safety Case structure, categories, acceptance (Clauses 7–8)

### 15.1 Safety Case parts (7.1, Fig. 6)
| Part | Content |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Definition of system** — system/subsystem/equipment definition incl. version numbers and modification status of all requirements/design/application documentation; delivery sheet/release note with current+previous versions on re-issue |
| 2 | **Quality Management Report (QMR)** — evidence of QMS effectiveness per 5.2 |
| 3 | **Safety Management Report (SMR)** — compliance with all safety-management elements per 5.3 over the whole life cycle |
| 4 | **Technical Safety Report (TSR)** — see below |
| 5 | **Related Safety Cases** — references to safety cases the system depends on; all their SRACs either **fulfilled** or **carried forward** into TSR §5; versions of the generic products/applications reported |
| 6 | **Conclusion** — summarised evidence + safety argument, subject to the application conditions |
Supporting documents may be referenced (not embedded) but must be available to the ISA.

### 15.2 Technical Safety Report sections (7.2, Fig. 7)
- **§1 Introduction:** design overview; claimed safety principles/extent per this standard; **basis standards and issues**; rule for older standard issues in modifications (justified, cost/disproportion considerations).
- **§2 Assurance of correct functional operation (fault-free):** 2.1 architecture description; 2.2 interfaces — **man-machine** (operator; configuration; maintenance) and **system** (internal: clean/dirty areas, buses, communication links, monitoring/diagnostics; external: sensors, actuators, links, test/monitoring provision, expansion); 2.3 fulfilment of the system requirements specification; 2.4 fulfilment of the safety requirements specification; 2.5 correct HW functionality (RAMS aspects, fault-free); 2.6 correct SW functionality — **EN 50128 mandatory**, results of the SW validation report included/referenced; HW/SW interaction (dependence, sequencing, response times, self-tests, health monitoring, data acquisition, graceful degradation, negation methods).
- **§3 Effects of faults (→ Annex B):** demonstration that specified safety requirements incl. the **quantified safety target** still hold under random HW faults; which technical measures reduce residual risk from possible systematic faults; demonstration that lower-SIL / Basic Integrity / non-safety functions **cannot reduce the safety of the overall system** (interface analysis → separation). Mandatory subheadings: 3.1 single faults; 3.2 independence of items; 3.3 detection; 3.4 action after detection; 3.5 multiple faults; 3.6 defence against systematic faults.
- **§4 Operation with external influences (→ 6.4, EN 50125-1/3):** 4.1 climatic; 4.2 mechanical; 4.3 altitude; 4.4 electrical; **4.5 protection against unauthorized access** — (1) physical: access levels (who/why/how + required skill-level criteria), protective measures (accidental authorised / intentional unauthorized), protections beyond the equipment (housing/location/accessibility), equipment housing (covers, mounting, seals, electrical/mechanical coding, firmware recognition); (2) **IT-security**: evaluation of threats to safety functions and the protection achieved; 4.6 more severe conditions (condensation, pollution, frost, solar/fire heating, plants/insects/animals, conductive dust, extreme temperatures). **The Safety Case is valid only within the specified external-influence envelope** — outside, safety is not assured unless special measures; systems should, so far as reasonably practicable, remain **safe** (not functional) even outside limits.
- **§5 SRACs:** rules/conditions/constraints for application — configuration of programmable systems; manufacturing/installation/testing/commissioning precautions; maintenance and fault-finding rules; operating instructions; safety warnings; EMC precautions (susceptibility + emission); modification & eventual decommissioning info; safety justification of support equipment (test/maintenance/configuration tools); **SW and configuration-data version compatibility**. 5.1 system configuration & build (config tools/procedures; version control; simulation; config-data spec; system build settings — application/interface/initialisation/maintenance; production testing; data preparation-tool version). 5.2 SRACs for operation/maintenance/monitoring (operational status: start-up [default conditions, init period, self-checks, manual intervention, output state, post-fire/unauthorized-entry precautions]; normal operation [cycle times, non-data routines, fault disclosure]; changeover [cold/hot standby, module-change reaction]; shut-down [defaults, graceful degradation, safety aspects, influences on other systems]; maintenance levels [1st/2nd line by maintainer, 2nd line by supplier]; periodic maintenance [training, accessibility, modularity, interchangeability, spares, storage]; maintenance aids [diagnostics, fault-message interpretation]). 5.3 SRACs for decommissioning/disposal (incl. phased replacement while the railway continues).
- **§6 Safety qualification test results** (5.3.12 evidence).
- **Basic Integrity TSR:** reduced structure; minimum coverage — fulfilment of system/safety requirements (e.g. via validation report); TFFR justification; environmental conditions + SRACs; functions included in safety qualification tests.

### 15.3 GPSC / GASC / SASC (7.3–7.5)
- **GPSC:** application-independent analyses; safety process typically complete through **phase 7 (manufacture)** with validation of the first manufactured item; type testing incl. environmental stress screening; plans/SRACs drafted for installation, commissioning, O&M, in-service data assessment, decommissioning.
- **GASC:** application-**dependent** context; process interrupted at phase 7 (first-equipment manufacture); adds a prepared, validated **application design process** (procedures + tools, e.g. interlocking generic data preparation) — evidence for the class of stations/applications.
- **SASC:** the specific application incl. physical implementation; covers **(a) application design** (correct specifications/inputs; deployed data correctness; compliance with generic SRACs) and **(b) physical implementation** (manufacturing/installation records compliant with SRACs; all data-preparation results with analysis/testing; on-site tests & commissioning proving **as-built = as-designed**). May be one combined case or two separate (application design / physical realisation), each structured per 7.1/7.2. Class-based reuse: GASC defines the configuration rules; each SASC written on its basis; either GASC or SASC **shall justify the safety of the configuration process**.
- **Dependencies (7.5):** reuse of accepted generic assessments by reference (Figs. 8/9 examples: ERTMS line SASC embracing a differently-developed IXL and ATC; station IXL SASC reusing GASC for a signal-actuator GP). Qualification-test evidence from the first specific application updates the GP/GA/SA cases.

### 15.4 Acceptance evidence and process (8.1)
Documentary evidence = system requirements specification + risk assessment outcomes + safety requirements specification + **Safety Case** + **ISA report**. Acceptance granted by the railway duty holder subject to legal-framework conditions. **Cross-acceptance** (of accepted generic products/applications) based on existing ISA — **no cross-acceptance for Specific Applications**.

## 16. Software safety integrity (EN 50128 link)

- Software of safety-related systems **shall comply with EN 50128** (5.3.8, 7.2 §2.6; Safety Plan shall reference it; SW support tools per EN 50128, not Clause 6.3).
- SIL inheritance: "the SIL adopted by EN 50128 for the safety-related software is the same as the function SIL" (A.4.3.3); **EN 50128 SIL 0 satisfies Basic Integrity software requirements** (A.4.3.3 Note). UPIC configurations are **not** EN 50128 software — the Annex F process covers them (but SW on soft cores, and high-level synthesis coding, are EN 50128 scope).
- All per-phase software technique tables (rigor classes T1–T3, M-classes, **ES** coding-standard requirements, etc.) live in **EN 50128** — EN 50129 references but does not restate them.
- **COTS/pre-existing SW**: via 6.2 (COTS equipment rules incl. full-rate pessimism, black-box integration, update freeze strategy) and EN 50128 provisions; tool dependencies → 6.3.

## 17. Common cause, probabilistic assumptions, safety-argument validity (collected)

- **CCF provisions:** forced CCF analysis for every AND gate (A.4.3.4/.5; B.3.5.1); diversity mandated between independent functions protecting the same hazard; physical influence classes A–D modelled (B.3.2); secondary independence allowed only for checker items; identical-redundancy architectures acknowledged as systematically-CCF-prone (TFFR not split); development/HR organisational independence as systematic CCF defence (A.4.3.6).
- **Probabilistic validity conditions (B.2.2):** constant rates; detection+negation implemented for combination-hazardous faults; negligible CCF; FR·SDT ≪ 1; conservative SDR usage; stress-profile-based FRs.
- **Quantified target verification chain:** risk assessment → THR → TFFR (Table A.1 → SIL) → FR apportionment → FTA/Markov check that calculated FFR ≤ TFFR incl. detection/negation modelling via SDT and the B-formulas → periodic-test intervals from (B.5) → in-service monitoring (5.3.9) closes the loop.

## 19. Practical silver bullets / cross-standard hooks (for RAMS engineering)

- Word-level formula pack: 2oo2 worst case — FFR = 2·FR²·SDT; test-interval design — T₁ ≤ TFFR/(FR_A·FR_B); Mü8004 conservative SDT bound with k from 1 (2oo2) to 0.5 (2oo3); **FR ≤ 2×10⁻⁶/h escapes triple-fault measures**; power-off ≤ 400×T_d with 20× reliability bonus.
- THR machinery is deliberately delegated to **EN 50126-2** (hourglass, apportionment formulas, GAME/MEM/ALARP acceptance) — EN 50129 fixes the **SIL table (A.1) and the technical evidence regime** (Annexes B/C/E + Safety Case).
- Software carries no separate technique requirements here: **check EN 50128** for T1–T3, SW M-class measures, ES coding standards; communication safety **EN 50159**; quantitative environmental/EMC envelopes from EN 50125-1/-3, EN 50121-3/-4, EN 50124-1, EN 50155; Markov/FTA/RBD methodology from EN 61025/61165/61078; component rates from IEC 61709/FMD-2013.
